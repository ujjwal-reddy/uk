Act I · From orbit

An atmosphere carrying more than it can hold.

The planet is absorbing carbon faster than it can lock away. Heavy industry, deep borewell extraction, fossil-fuel combustion, synthetic nitrogen fertilizers and deforestation each push a stream of CO₂ into a thin shell that was never built to store it — while the soils that should be sinking that carbon are stripped of the organic matter that lets them hold it.

  • Industrial & thermal combustionContinuous point-source CO₂, SOx and particulate loading over semi-arid districts.
  • Deep borewell extractionFalling water tables force deeper bores, higher energy draw and rising salt concentration in irrigation water.
  • Chemical fertilizer dependenceUrea-heavy regimes acidify the root zone, collapse microbial life and vent N₂O — ~273× the warming potential of CO₂.
  • Deforestation & canopy lossRemoving standing biomass deletes the fastest natural sink and exposes topsoil to wind and heat erosion.
  • Open residue burningStubble and prunings burned in the open release stored carbon in hours instead of banking it for centuries.

The land · Anantapur & Sri Sathya Sai

India's second-driest districts, and the reason CE² exists.

CE² was founded inside the crisis it is built to solve. These two districts sit in a deep rain shadow — an agricultural economy running on a rainfall budget that no longer arrives, on soils going saline and acidic at the same time.

Act IV · Below the surface

How the carbon actually gets restored.

Carbon removal only counts if the carbon stays removed. This is the path a single tonne takes at CE² — out of the air, through the kiln, into the soil, and held there on a timescale that has nothing to do with the next growing season.

Why the soil holds it

Pyrolysis rearranges the carbon in biomass into fused aromatic rings. Soil microbes that would strip fresh organic matter in a season cannot break that structure down — so the carbon sits in the pore network for centuries instead of returning to the atmosphere. The same porosity that makes it permanent is what makes it agronomically useful.

DurableCentury-to-millennium residence time in soil, not a decadal one.
